We always had an old computer at home, but I was 9 years old when my dad bought a decent laptop on which he had gotten a game installed.

My brother and I used to fight over it so we could play Road Rash.

  <RoadRash />
</Collapsible>

That laptop was my whole world. Eventually, I got curious about what else I could do on it. 

In 4th grade, I figured out how to make animated PowerPoint presentations all by myself — no guides, no tutorials, just clicking around. Within a few months, I was demoing animations at school.

It sounds small, but that feeling of being capable with a machine, understanding it, and creating something from scratch is where everything started.

From there, I went deeper — web development, WordPress, YouTube tutorials. Long hours of trying things just to see what would happen. 

Growing up in Akola wasn't just screens, though. It was running around with my dog, Bela, and hanging out with a tight group of school friends who made everyday life fun before everything became about careers and engineering.

By the time I had to choose what to study, there wasn't really a decision to make. I just knew.

When I finished school, unlike most, I enrolled in a Diploma in Computer Engineering at Government Polytechnic Pune.

I skipped chemistry, biology, and physics to learn computer science from day one. Building real projects gave me a strong foundation for engineering college later. I don't know if I'd make the same choice again, but it turned out well.

I was 16 when I moved to Pune. I had never lived away from home. 

The first few weeks felt like total freedom — a new city, no curfew, no one watching. 

Then came my first bad day. I came back to an empty room: no one to sit with, no one to rant to, no food waiting, and no familiar smells from the kitchen. I had to cook, do laundry, and solve my own problems, then wake up and do it again.

Six years after leaving home at 16, I graduated from Vishwakarma Institute of Technology in 2025.

Nobody taught me to code. Teachers and classes did the formality, but the real learning happened differently.

The hard part of coding is starting, and the harder part is staying grounded. Early on, everyone thinks they're an expert. Realizing how little I actually knew took time.

Talent or opportunities didn't pull me through — consistency did. Staying up until 2 am became normal, and building something every day was what I looked forward to.

I've participated in over 20 hackathons and won half of them. You learn to build fast, think under pressure, and ship something in a room full of competitors.

SIH 2023 was different. We built *NyaySathi* — a legal AI assistant for marginalized communities.

We failed a hundred times before anything worked. We didn't eat, our first two evaluations were terrible, and some of us cried. But because we were best friends, the energy shifted, and things started falling into place. 

At 10 pm, they announced our name as the winners. I don't think about that night because of the trophy, but because of what we pulled off as a team.

I joined TEDxVITPune on Day 1 of college. At first, I kept to myself, unsure of what I was doing there.

It quickly became more than a club. The thrill of grinding for months with a team to pull off a massive campus event got under my skin. When my pre-final year came and most batchmates shifted focus to placements, I stayed. The people at TEDx had become my closest friends.

In my final year, I became the licensee and lead organizer, leading a team of seven close friends with nine editions of history behind us. It was the hardest thing I'd ever done — weekly setbacks, plans falling apart, and breaking down under pressure. But we held each other up.

We produced the 10th edition: *Dimes of Dynamism*.

In September 2025, I passed the baton to the next team. A part of me still hasn't let go; TEDx was my real home on campus.

Today, I'm an Associate Software Engineer at [Workato](/workato), working on connectors, MCP, and AI.

Outside of work, I build side projects, contribute to open source, and help my brother build his business. Most things I start never get finished, and I've learned to be honest with myself about where my genuine curiosity lies.

When I'm immersed in something, time vanishes. I'll sit at my desk for 12–15 hours, look up at 3 am, and realize I never felt tired. That kind of absorption is the most honest thing I know about myself.

I used to think grades were everything. Then I realized doing well inside a room doesn't matter once you step outside of it.

What I'm chasing is simple: financial freedom to choose work freely, taking care of the people I love, giving back without needing profit, and earning respect among people who care about the craft.

I'm not there yet. 

Right now, I live in Akola and work remotely. I hit the gym, cook most nights, watch cricket, and hang out with family. After dinner, I'm back at my desk until I finish something or break it completely. I'm 22, still figuring it out.
